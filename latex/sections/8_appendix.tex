\section{Statistical comparisons}
\label{app:stats}

Paired talk-level tests show that the gains of the routed system over
Merged-1M hold across individual talks. Table~\ref{tab:stats} reports every
paired comparison. The exact permutation test swaps the outputs of two systems
within each talk across all 1,024 combinations and recomputes the pooled
metric, giving a minimum attainable two-sided $p$-value of 0.002. Significance
claims rest on this test. With ten talks, the percentile bootstrap can disagree
with it near the boundary: for Merged-10k in run 2 and for the MT-BLEU of
similarity routing in run 3, the interval excludes zero while $p\ge0.08$.
Confidence intervals come from 4,000 stratified bootstrap resamples of five
ACL and five ESO talks. Removing any single talk preserves all three
advantages of similarity routing over Merged-1M in run 1, with gains of at
least 3.28 TERM\_ACC points, 2.00 BLEU, and 1.99 MT-BLEU.

\section{Router configuration}
\label{app:config}

Table~\ref{tab:config} lists the routing and retrieval settings. The
language-model router adds a single parameter to the shared settings, the
switch rate $\rho$. The similarity router, used as a baseline, embeds the
context and each prototype with BGE-M3~\citep{chen-etal-2024-m3}, reading at
most the last 128 tokens of $c_t$, and extends each prototype with its Chinese
translation. It keeps a confirmed slice that changes only when another slice
leads both the runner-up and the confirmed slice by fixed margins in three
consecutive updates and not within 30 seconds of the previous change, and it
pins that slice in the working set. We selected these thresholds on four short
segments drawn from the evaluation talks before running the complete streams.
Because both routers update on wall-clock time, each ran alone on its server.

\section{Router diagnostics}
\label{app:router}

Open-loop replays compare the two deployed routers on identical inputs. Each
replay feeds the saved translations of run 1 to both routers without decoding
again and builds the context from the last four translated chunks as in
Section~\ref{sec:adaptive-memory}. As in the live system, context scores
refresh at most once every 5 seconds of audio, the similarity router applies
its confirmation rule and pins its confirmed slice, and the language-model
router takes one filter step per refresh with $\rho=10^{-3}$.
Table~\ref{tab:router-diag} reports how often the correct slice is in the
four-slice working set. Replayed on the emission times of run 1, the
similarity router makes 19 switches, as it did live, and covers 96.2\% of
chunks against 96.9\% live.

\paragraph{Topic filter.}
The filter turns noisy per-update scores into a stable ranking without
thresholds. Updated at every chunk, letter probabilities alone keep the
correct slice among the top four in 90.5\% of chunks, and the filtered
posterior in 98.7\%. Coverage stays between 98.4\% and 98.9\% as $\rho$ ranges
from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-1}$, so the single free parameter needs no tuning. Raw
similarity covers 85.0\% under the same updates, and the confirmation rule
and pin raise the similarity router to 97.0\%.

\paragraph{Short topic shifts.}
The posterior follows topics that alternate every 15 to 300 seconds. For each
length in Table~\ref{tab:router-diag}, we cut the saved translations of the NLP
and medicine talks into segments of that length and interleave them into one
40-segment stream. At every length, the median segment starts with the
incoming slice already in the working set of the language-model router, and
the slice reaches the top of the posterior after a median of 7.7 seconds. The
similarity router waits for three consecutive leads and a 30-second cooldown,
so in segments of one minute or longer it confirms the incoming slice only
after a median of 27 to 46 seconds.

\paragraph{Other target languages.}
The router reads German and Japanese output as well as Chinese. We replay the
German and Japanese RASST translations of the same ten talks and change only
the target language named in the router prompt.

\paragraph{Close domains.}
Finer slices separate the two routers further. The same 4B model splits the
NLP and medicine catalogs into eight subtopics each: it proposes subtopics from
400 terms outside the evaluation glossaries and assigns every term by letter
probability. With the eight other topics, this yields 24 slices. On the 2,047
gold term occurrences, the slice holding the term is in the working set for
82.7\% under the language-model router and 52.2\% under the similarity router,
against 97.3\% for the best four slices chosen per talk in hindsight.

\paragraph{Generated prototypes and late updates.}
Prototypes need not be written by hand. GPT-5.5 wrote one per slice from the
slice's glossary with every evaluation term removed; with per-chunk updates,
the language-model router then keeps the correct slice among its top four in
97.9\% of ten-talk chunks, against 98.7\% with the hand-written prototypes. The
RealSI streams use the generated prototypes, because the hand-written NLP
prototype names topics of the RealSI technology talk. The closed-loop runs of
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} scored each update before the chunk continued,
whereas the deployed worker can apply it one chunk later when audio arrives
faster than real time; replaying every update one chunk late lowers the
coverage of the language-model router by at most 0.33 points on the replays
of Table~\ref{tab:router-diag}.
